The fact-checker

Search is confidently wrong a lot of the time. Here is what this codebase does about it.

A real result, from a real run

Somebody searched for "show me a car". Here is what actually came back.

12
candidate segments returned

Ranked by how closely each one matched. Every one of them scored well enough to show.

1
confirmed on inspection

A second look agreed: yes, that is a car.

2
rejected on inspection

Confident, high-scoring, and wrong. Two of the top three.

Nothing was broken. That is search working normally.

⚠️
A number worth carrying around

If you build anything that searches by meaning — documents, images, video, support tickets — expect a meaningful share of confident, well-ranked, wrong results. Plan for it in the design instead of being surprised by it in production.

Why it goes wrong

Searching here is not looking for words. Every video segment is turned into an embedding — a long list of numbers describing roughly what it looks like.

📍
Everything gets a position

Each segment, and your typed question too. Similar-looking things land near each other.

📏
Search means "what is nearest?"

Not "what contains this word". That is why plain English works at all.

🎭
Nearest is not the same as right

A red van sits very close to a red car. Nearest always returns something — even when nothing matches.

💡
This kind of search can never say "no"

Ask for the twelve nearest segments and you get twelve, always — even if your subject appears nowhere in the footage. The ranking has no notion of "none of these are it". Something else has to supply that judgement.

Sending it to the fact-checker

A newspaper does not print a reporter's claim because it sounds plausible. Someone checks it against the source first.

So the agent takes the frame nearest each top hit and asks the vision model a much narrower question. This second pass is called a critic.

CODE 

    prompt = (
        "You are verifying a video search result.\n"
        f'The user asked for: "{query}".\n'
        "Looking only at this frame, decide whether it genuinely shows that, "
        "and say which of these are visible:\n"
        + "\n".join(f"- {item}" for item in checkable)
        + "\nBe strict: if something is not clearly visible, it is not present."
    )
PLAIN ENGLISH

Build the prompt that goes to the vision model alongside the picture.

Give it one job, and say so first. It is not chatting, it is checking.

Quote what the person actually asked for, word for word.

"Looking only at this frame" — no guessing from context, no benefit of the doubt.

Then ask it to go through a checklist rather than give a yes or no.

The checklist itself: each thing that must be visible, as its own line.

And the whole trick, in one sentence. Without it, models are agreeable and say yes.

That is the fact-checker.

"Be strict" is doing an enormous amount of work here. A model asked "is this a red car?" wants to be helpful. It has to be told that unhelpful is the correct answer when the picture does not support it.

Three verdicts, not two

CODE 

CONFIRMED = "Confirmed"
REJECTED = "Rejected"
UNVERIFIED = "Unverified"
PLAIN ENGLISH

The checker looked and agreed.

The checker looked and disagreed.

Nobody looked — it ran out of budget, or the check failed. Crucially not the same as "rejected", and the interface says so.

🤔
"Unknown" deserves its own answer

Collapsing "we did not check" into "it failed" is one of the most common ways software lies to people. Any time you design a status, ask whether it needs a third value for we do not know — it usually does.

Why not check all twelve?

Because each check is a full look at a picture by the vision model, and the model server does them one at a time.

CRITIC_MAX_CLIPS=3 How many results get a second look. Raise it for accuracy, lower it for speed — this one setting is the whole trade-off.
CRITIC_ENABLED Turn checking off entirely. Faster, and every result becomes Unverified rather than silently trusted.

A slow or failed check never sinks the search. It returns Unverified and the results still arrive.

⚖️
Accuracy has a price, and it is time

This is the shape of nearly every quality decision in AI systems. There is no setting that is simply "better" — there is a dial between fast and right, and someone has to choose where it sits for their situation.
